Lesson 5

Worked calculations

Nine worked examples, each a different kind of problem, spanning gases, liquids, solids and chemical reactions. Reveal each solution one step at a time — try the next step yourself before you click! Then try the variation underneath.

A strategy for every first-law problem

  1. Define the system. Draw a box around it. What crosses the boundary?
  2. Assign signs from the system's point of view: energy in (+), energy out (−).
  3. Find q — from mcΔT, nΔH, calorimetry, or "q = 0" if insulated.
  4. Find w — from −PextΔV, −ΔngasRT, or "w = 0" if ΔV = 0 or Pext = 0.
  5. Combine with ΔU = q + w, converting units first (L·atm → J; kJ ↔ J).
  6. Sanity check the sign and size. Did the system gain or lose energy? Does that make physical sense?

The examples

Example 1

Internal energy change from q and w

gas

A gas in a cylinder absorbs 250 J of heat from a hot plate and, at the same time, does 75 J of work pushing a piston outward. What is ΔU for the gas?

  1. System: the gas. Heat is absorbed → enters the system → q = +250 J.
  2. The gas does work on the surroundings → energy leaves → w = −75 J.
  3. ΔU = q + w = (+250 J) + (−75 J) = +175 J.
  4. ΔU = +175 J. The gas gained energy overall: it took in more as heat than it spent as work.
Try it: A system releases 120 J of heat while 340 J of work is done on it. Find ΔU (in J).
Example 2

Work of expansion against a constant external pressure

gas

A gas expands from 2.0 L to 5.5 L against a constant external pressure of 1.2 atm. Calculate the work in joules. What would the work be if the gas expanded into a vacuum instead?

  1. ΔV = V2 − V1 = 5.5 L − 2.0 L = +3.5 L (expansion, so positive).
  2. w = −PextΔV = −(1.2 atm)(3.5 L) = −4.2 L·atm.
  3. Convert: −4.2 L·atm × (101.325 J / 1 L·atm) = −426 J (−425.6 J).
  4. Into a vacuum, Pext = 0, so w = −(0)(3.5 L) = 0. The gas has nothing to push against, so it does no work — even though its volume grows by the same amount.
  5. w = −426 J against 1.2 atm; w = 0 into a vacuum. Work is a path function: same ΔV, different work.
Try it: A gas expands from 3.0 L to 8.0 L against a constant 0.80 atm. Find w in joules.
Example 3

Finding heat from ΔU during a compression

gas

A gas is compressed from 10.0 L to 4.0 L by a constant external pressure of 3.0 atm. During the process its internal energy increases by 1.20 kJ. Was heat absorbed or released, and how much?

  1. ΔV = 4.0 L − 10.0 L = −6.0 L (compression, so negative).
  2. w = −PextΔV = −(3.0 atm)(−6.0 L) = +18 L·atm = 18 × 101.325 J = +1824 J. Positive: the surroundings did work on the gas.
  3. Rearrange the first law: q = ΔU − w = 1200 J − 1824 J = −624 J.
  4. q = −624 J: the gas released 624 J of heat. The surroundings put 1824 J in as work; 1200 J stayed as internal energy and 624 J leaked out as heat.
Try it: A gas is compressed from 6.0 L to 2.0 L by a constant 2.5 atm, and ΔU = +500 J. Find q in J.
Example 4

Heating a solid: is the work really negligible?

solid

A 150 g aluminum block is heated from 25 °C to 95 °C at 1 atm. Find q, w and ΔU.
Data: cAl = 0.897 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹; density 2.70 g/cm³; aluminum's volume grows by 6.9 × 10⁻⁵ of its volume per °C.

  1. Heat: q = mcΔT = (150 g)(0.897 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹)(95 − 25 °C) = +9418 J ≈ +9.42 kJ.
  2. Volume change: V = 150 g ÷ 2.70 g/cm³ = 55.6 cm³. ΔV = (6.9 × 10⁻⁵ °C⁻¹)(55.6 cm³)(70 °C) = 0.27 cm³ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁴ L.
  3. Work: w = −PextΔV = −(1 atm)(2.7 × 10⁻⁴ L) = −2.7 × 10⁻⁴ L·atm × 101.325 = −0.027 J.
  4. ΔU = 9418 J + (−0.027 J) = 9418 J. The work is about 3 parts in a million of q!
  5. q = +9.42 kJ, w ≈ −0.03 J, ΔU ≈ +9.42 kJ. For solids (and liquids) heated without a phase change, ΔU ≈ q.
Try it: 250 g of iron (c = 0.449 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹) is heated from 20 °C to 80 °C. Find ΔU in kJ (treat w as negligible).
Example 5

Vaporizing a liquid: q, w and ΔU for boiling water

liquid → gas

1.00 mol of water boils at 100 °C and 1.00 atm. ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol. Find q, w and ΔU. Treat the steam as an ideal gas.

  1. Heat (constant P, phase change): q = nΔHvap = (1.00 mol)(40.7 kJ/mol) = +40.7 kJ.
  2. Volume change: Vgas = nRT/P = (1.00)(0.08206)(373.15)/(1.00) = 30.6 L. Vliquid = 18.0 g ÷ 0.958 g/mL ≈ 0.019 L — negligible. So ΔV ≈ +30.6 L.
  3. Work: w = −PΔV ≈ −nRT = −(1.00 mol)(8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(373.15 K) = −3102 J = −3.10 kJ. (Check: −30.6 L·atm × 101.325 = −3100 J ✓)
  4. ΔU = q + w = 40.7 kJ − 3.10 kJ = +37.6 kJ.
  5. q = +40.7 kJ, w = −3.10 kJ, ΔU = +37.6 kJ. About 92% of the heat goes into pulling the molecules apart (ΔU); 8% is spent pushing back the atmosphere to make room for the steam.
Try it: 2.00 mol of ethanol boils at 78 °C (351.15 K) and 1 atm; ΔHvap = 38.6 kJ/mol. Find ΔU in kJ.
Example 6

Melting a solid: when the atmosphere does work on the system

solid → liquid

1.00 mol (18.02 g) of ice melts at 0 °C and 1.00 atm. ΔHfus = 6.01 kJ/mol. Densities: ice 0.917 g/mL, liquid water 1.000 g/mL. Find q, w and ΔU.

  1. Heat: q = nΔHfus = (1.00 mol)(6.01 kJ/mol) = +6.01 kJ = +6010 J.
  2. Volumes: Vice = 18.02 g ÷ 0.917 g/mL = 19.65 mL; Vwater = 18.02 mL. ΔV = 18.02 − 19.65 = −1.63 mL = −1.63 × 10⁻³ L. The volume decreases — ice is unusual!
  3. Work: w = −PΔV = −(1.00 atm)(−1.63 × 10⁻³ L) = +1.63 × 10⁻³ L·atm × 101.325 = +0.165 J. Positive: the atmosphere pushes in on the shrinking sample.
  4. ΔU = 6010 J + 0.165 J = 6010 J.
  5. q = +6.01 kJ, w = +0.17 J, ΔU = +6.01 kJ. Compare with Example 5: |w| for boiling is ~19,000 times larger than for melting, because only boiling creates a gas.
Try it: About how many times larger is q than w for melting ice? (Use the values above; within 5% counts.)
Example 7

A reaction that produces a gas: ΔU vs ΔH

reaction

Zinc dissolves in hydrochloric acid in an open flask at 25 °C and 1 atm:
Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)    ΔH = −153.9 kJ/mol
For a reaction that produces 0.500 mol H2, find q, w and ΔU.

  1. System: the reacting chemicals. At constant P, q = ΔH. For 0.500 mol: q = (0.500 mol)(−153.9 kJ/mol) = −76.9 kJ (exothermic).
  2. Work: only the gas matters. Δngas = +0.500 mol (H2 produced; aqueous species and Zn(s) take up negligible volume). w = −PΔV = −ΔngasRT = −(0.500)(8.314)(298.15) = −1239 J = −1.24 kJ.
  3. ΔU = q + w = −76.9 kJ + (−1.24 kJ) = −78.2 kJ.
  4. q = −76.9 kJ, w = −1.24 kJ, ΔU = −78.2 kJ. The system loses energy two ways: heat to the solution and work pushing back the air. ΔU and ΔH differ by ΔngasRT.
Try it: Propane burns at 25 °C: C3H8(g) + 5 O2(g) → 3 CO2(g) + 4 H2O(l). Find w in kJ per mole of propane. (Watch which species are gases!)
Example 8

Bomb calorimetry: measuring ΔU directly

reaction

0.500 g of glucose (C6H12O6, 180.16 g/mol) is burned in a bomb calorimeter whose total heat capacity is 10.0 kJ/°C. The temperature rises by 0.780 °C. Find ΔU of combustion in kJ/mol.

  1. Heat absorbed by the calorimeter (surroundings): qcal = CcalΔT = (10.0 kJ/°C)(0.780 °C) = +7.80 kJ.
  2. Energy conservation: the reaction (system) released that heat, so qrxn = −qcal = −7.80 kJ.
  3. The bomb is rigid, so w = 0 and ΔU = qV = −7.80 kJ for this sample.
  4. Moles of glucose: 0.500 g ÷ 180.16 g/mol = 2.775 × 10⁻³ mol. ΔU = −7.80 kJ ÷ 2.775 × 10⁻³ mol = −2810 kJ/mol.
  5. ΔUcomb = −2.81 × 10³ kJ/mol. (For glucose, C6H12O6(s) + 6 O2(g) → 6 CO2(g) + 6 H2O(l), Δngas = 0, so ΔH ≈ ΔU here.) This is the energy your body gets from glucose!
Try it: In the same calorimeter, a sample's combustion raises the temperature by 1.65 °C. Find ΔU for the reaction in kJ (for the sample, not per mole).
Example 9

Same change, two paths: one-step vs reversible isothermal expansion

gas

1.00 mol of an ideal gas at 298 K expands isothermally from 10.0 L to 20.0 L. Find w, q and ΔU if (a) it expands in one step against a constant Pext equal to the final pressure, and (b) it expands reversibly. (Lesson 6 explains reversible processes in detail.)

  1. Either path: ideal gas, constant T → ΔU = 0, so q = −w.
  2. (a) Pfinal = nRT/V2 = (1.00)(0.08206)(298)/(20.0) = 1.22 atm. w = −PextΔV = −(1.22 atm)(10.0 L) = −12.2 L·atm = −1239 J. So q = +1239 J.
  3. (b) Reversible isothermal: w = −nRT ln(V2/V1) = −(1.00)(8.314)(298) ln(2) = −1717 J. So q = +1717 J.
  4. (a) w = −1.24 kJ, q = +1.24 kJ; (b) w = −1.72 kJ, q = +1.72 kJ; ΔU = 0 for both. Same initial and final states, same ΔU — but the reversible path gets 39% more work out of the gas.
Try it: 2.00 mol of ideal gas expands reversibly and isothermally at 300 K from 5.0 L to 15.0 L. Find w in kJ.

Comparing the results

Put the examples side by side and a clear pattern appears: work is significant only when a gas is involved.
