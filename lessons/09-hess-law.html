Lesson 9

Hess's law and calculating ΔH

Many enthalpy changes are hard or impossible to measure directly — you can't easily burn carbon to make only CO, or turn graphite into diamond in a calorimeter. Because enthalpy is a state function, we don't have to: we can calculate ΔH along any convenient path.

In this lesson

  • State Hess's law and explain why it works.
  • Apply the rules for manipulating thermochemical equations.
  • Calculate ΔH by every standard method: calorimetry (constant P and constant V), Hess's law, enthalpies of formation, bond enthalpies, phase changes, temperature correction and Born–Haber cycles.
  • Choose the right method for solids, liquids, gases and solutions.

Hess's law

Hess's law (1840) If a process can be written as the sum of several steps, the enthalpy change for the overall process is the sum of the enthalpy changes of the steps.1,2

Germain Hess found this experimentally in 1840, before the first law was understood.2 Today we see it as a direct consequence of H being a state function (Lesson 5): ΔH depends only on the start and end, so any route between them — real or imaginary — gives the same total.1,3

The rules

A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with physical states and its ΔH. You can manipulate it like this:1,3

1
Reverse the equation → change the sign of ΔH.
H₂O(l) → H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g)   ΔH = +285.8 kJ
is the reverse of formation (−285.8 kJ).
2
Multiply the equation by a factor → multiply ΔH by the same factor.
2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(l)   ΔH = 2(−285.8) = −571.6 kJ
ΔH is extensive.
3
Add equations → add their ΔH values. Cancel any species that appears on both sides in the same physical state.
4
Physical states must match. H₂O(l) and H₂O(g) are different substances for enthalpy: H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) has ΔH = +44.0 kJ/mol at 25 °C.4
5
The steps don't have to be real. Any path works, even one through imaginary intermediates, because only the end points matter.
6
Elements in their standard states have ΔHf° = 0 (e.g. O₂(g), C(graphite), Na(s)). This is the reference point that lets us tabulate formation enthalpies.1,9

Every way to calculate ΔH

Hess's law underlies almost every method. Here they all are, with the kinds of systems each one suits.

1Constant-pressure (coffee-cup) calorimetry — solutions, dissolving, neutralization

At constant pressure, ΔH = qP. The reaction's heat is absorbed by the solution, so qrxn = −qsolution = −m cΔT.7

Example

Neutralization: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq)

solutions

50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl and 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at 22.0 °C, are mixed in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The temperature rises to 28.7 °C. Assume the solution has density 1.00 g/mL and c = 4.184 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Find ΔH per mole of water formed.

  1. qsoln = m cΔT = (100.0 g)(4.184 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹)(6.7 °C) = 2803 J.
  2. The reaction released this heat: qrxn = −2803 J.
  3. Moles of water formed = moles of HCl = (0.0500 L)(1.00 mol/L) = 0.0500 mol.
  4. ΔH = −2803 J ÷ 0.0500 mol = −56 kJ/mol, close to the accepted −55.8 kJ/mol.4
Try it: Dissolving 4.00 g of NaOH(s) (0.100 mol) in water gives a solution of total mass 104.0 g that warms by 10.2 °C. Find ΔHsoln in kJ/mol.

2Constant-volume (bomb) calorimetry, then ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT — combustion of solids, liquids and gases

A bomb calorimeter measures qV = ΔU. To convert to ΔH, add the PV term, which only matters for gases:3,7

ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT Δngas = moles of gaseous products − moles of gaseous reactants
Example

Combustion of methane

gas

A bomb calorimeter gives ΔU = −885.4 kJ/mol for CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) at 298 K. Find ΔH.

  1. Count gases: products 1 (CO₂; water is liquid), reactants 3 → Δngas = 1 − 3 = −2.
  2. ΔngasRT = (−2)(8.314 × 10⁻³ kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(298 K) = −4.96 kJ/mol.
  3. ΔH = −885.4 + (−4.96) = −890.4 kJ/mol. For a solid or liquid fuel that makes no net gas, ΔH ≈ ΔU.

3Hess's law — combining known equations (any phase)

Use the puzzle tool above: reverse, multiply and add given thermochemical equations until they sum to the target.1 This is how enthalpies of reactions that can't be run cleanly (CO formation, graphite → diamond) are found.

4Standard enthalpies of formation — the most general method

Imagine every reaction going through the elements: first break the reactants down into elements (−ΣΔHf° of reactants), then build the products from them (+ΣΔHf° of products). Hess's law gives:1,4

ΔH°rxn = Σ n ΔHf°(products) − Σ m ΔHf°(reactants)

5Average bond enthalpies — quick estimates for gas-phase reactions

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming bonds releases it. For reactions of gases:6

ΔH ≈ Σ D(bonds broken) − Σ D(bonds formed)
What is D? D is the bond enthalpy (bond dissociation enthalpy): the enthalpy change to break one mole of a particular bond, with everything in the gas phase, into separate gaseous atoms.6 For example, H–H(g) → 2 H(g), D(H–H) = +436 kJ/mol. D is always positive because breaking a bond always takes energy. That's why the D values of bonds broken are added (energy in) and those of bonds formed are subtracted (energy released).

Bond enthalpies are averages over many molecules, so this gives an estimate (usually within a few percent) and works only when everything is a gas — it ignores the intermolecular forces in liquids and solids.6

6Heating and phase changes — q = nCPΔT and q = nΔHtransition

For physical changes, split the process into heating steps and phase changes and add them — Hess's law with each step measured separately. Lesson 8 has an interactive calculator for water.10 Example: warming 18.0 g of ice from −10 °C to steam at 110 °C takes 0.38 + 6.01 + 7.53 + 40.7 + 0.36 = 55.0 kJ.

7Correcting ΔH to a different temperature (Kirchhoff's law)

Tables usually give ΔH at 25 °C. At another temperature, follow a Hess cycle: cool the reactants to 25 °C, react, then warm the products back. The result is:3

ΔH(T2) = ΔH(T1) + ΔCP(T2 − T1) ΔCP = CP(products) − CP(reactants), assumed roughly constant

8Born–Haber cycle — lattice enthalpy of ionic solids

The lattice enthalpy of an ionic solid can't be measured directly. Instead we build the solid from its elements by two routes — directly (ΔHf°), or through gaseous atoms and ions — and use Hess's law to find the missing step.6,8
