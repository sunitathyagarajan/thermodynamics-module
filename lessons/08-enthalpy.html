Lesson 8

Enthalpy

Most chemistry happens in open beakers at constant atmospheric pressure. Enthalpy, H, is the energy bookkeeping tool built for exactly those conditions: at constant pressure, the heat a process absorbs or releases is its enthalpy change.

In this lesson

  • Define enthalpy and show why ΔH = qP.
  • Explain how ΔH is measured for solids, liquids and gases, and how they differ.
  • Recognise the different forms of enthalpy change (formation, combustion, fusion, solution, bond, lattice…).
  • Calculate enthalpy changes for heating, cooling and changes of phase.

What is enthalpy?

H = U + PV enthalpy = internal energy + pressure × volume

Enthalpy adds to the internal energy the "PV" energy the system needs to make room for itself against the surrounding pressure.1,3 Because U, P and V are all state functions, H is a state function too (Lesson 5): ΔH depends only on the initial and final states.1,3 Like U, its absolute value can't be measured — only changes, ΔH = Hfinal − Hinitial. It is extensive: double the amount of material and you double ΔH. Its units are joules (usually kJ or kJ/mol).

How is enthalpy related to q?

Start from the first law and add one condition — constant pressure, with only pressure–volume work:1,3

  1. First law: ΔU = q + w
  2. If the only work is expansion work against the constant pressure, w = −PΔV. So ΔU = qP − PΔV.
  3. Rearrange: qP = ΔU + PΔV.
  4. At constant P, ΔH = Δ(U + PV) = ΔU + PΔV. Therefore:
ΔH = qP at constant pressure, when the only work is pressure–volume work
Key facts about ΔH
  • Sign: ΔH < 0 is exothermic (heat released); ΔH > 0 is endothermic (heat absorbed).1
  • Extensive: 2 CH4 burning releases twice the heat of 1 CH4.
  • Reversing a process reverses the sign: ΔHfreezing = −ΔHmelting.
  • Physical states matter: forming H2O(l) releases 44.0 kJ/mol more than forming H2O(g) at 25 °C.7
  • The condition matters: ΔH = q only at constant pressure with no non-PV work. A battery doing electrical work, for example, releases less heat than its ΔH.3

Can we measure enthalpy for solids, liquids and gases?

Yes — we can measure ΔH for processes involving any phase (never an absolute H). The basic method is always calorimetry: measure the heat at constant pressure, or measure it at constant volume and correct it.2,3 What differs between phases is how big the PV term is, and therefore how different ΔH is from ΔU.

Enthalpy of different phases

For the same substance at the same temperature, the phases are ordered Hsolid < Hliquid < Hgas: energy must be added to loosen molecules from a solid into a liquid, and much more to separate them completely into a gas.4 That's why ΔHvap is much larger than ΔHfus (40.7 vs 6.01 kJ/mol for water). Because H is a state function, the enthalpies of the phase changes fit together like a closed loop:

ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap (both at the same temperature)
ΔHfreeze = −ΔHfus  ·  ΔHcondense = −ΔHvap

Example (sublimation of ice at 0 °C): ΔHfus = 6.01 kJ/mol and ΔHvap at 0 °C = 45.05 kJ/mol, so ΔHsub = 51.06 kJ/mol.6 (Using the 100 °C value, 40.7 kJ/mol, would underestimate it — ΔHvap depends on temperature; Lesson 9 shows how to correct for this.)
