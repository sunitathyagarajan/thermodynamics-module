Lesson 11

Spontaneity: the universe keeps score

Why does ice melt at 10 °C but not at −10 °C? Why do some endothermic reactions happen on their own? The answer is to count entropy in both the system and its surroundings — the total for the universe decides.

In this lesson

  • Define a spontaneous process and explain why enthalpy alone can't predict spontaneity.
  • Calculate the entropy change of a system for phase changes, heating, gas expansion and reactions.
  • Calculate the entropy change of the surroundings, ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys/T.
  • Use ΔSuniv to decide whether a process is spontaneous, at equilibrium or nonspontaneous.
  • Explain the factors that increase or decrease entropy, and how they affect spontaneity.

What is a spontaneous process?

A spontaneous process happens on its own once it starts, without continuous help from outside. The reverse of a spontaneous process is nonspontaneous: it only happens if we keep supplying energy or work.1

SpontaneousNonspontaneous (the reverse)
Ice melts on a warm dayWater freezes on a warm day
Iron rusts in moist airRust turns back into iron and oxygen
Heat flows from hot coffee to the cool roomHeat flows from the room into the coffee
Salt dissolves in waterSalt crystallises from a dilute solution
Gas expands into a vacuumGas gathers into one corner
Spontaneous ≠ fastDiamond turning into graphite is spontaneous, but so slow you'll never see it. Thermodynamics says whether a process can happen, not how fast.1
Spontaneous ≠ exothermicIce melting and an instant cold pack (NH₄NO₃ dissolving) are endothermic, yet spontaneous. Enthalpy alone can't predict spontaneity — we need entropy.1,3

Entropy of the system and the surroundings

The second law is about the universe — the system plus its surroundings (Lesson 2):3

ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr

Standard molar entropies, S°

Unlike enthalpy, entropy has an absolute zero point (the third law: a perfect crystal at 0 K has S = 0), so tables list absolute standard molar entropies, S°, at 1 bar and 25 °C.3,6 For pure substances (elements and compounds) they are always positive. Values for ions in water are measured relative to H⁺(aq) = 0, so some — such as Mg²⁺(aq), −138.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ — are negative.6

Calculating ΔS of the system

Entropy is defined through reversible heat (Lesson 7): for a process at constant temperature, ΔS = qrev/T.2,8 From that one definition come all the formulas you need:

ProcessFormulaNotes
Any reversible process at constant TΔS = qrev / TThe definition; T in kelvin2
Phase change at its transition temperatureΔS = ΔHtrans / TtransMelting, boiling: reversible at Ttrans3,5
Heating or cooling (no phase change)ΔS = m c ln(T2/T1) = nCP ln(T2/T1)Constant pressure5
Ideal gas at constant TΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) = nR ln(P1/P2)Expansion: ΔS > 08
Chemical reactionΔS°rxn = Σn S°(products) − Σm S°(reactants)Same pattern as Hess's law3
Example 1

Phase change: boiling water

liquid → gas

Find ΔS for vaporizing 1.00 mol of water at its normal boiling point, 100.0 °C (ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol).

  1. Convert to kelvin: T = 100.0 + 273.15 = 373.15 K. Convert to joules: ΔH = 40 700 J/mol.
  2. ΔS = ΔHvap/Tb = 40 700 / 373.15 = +109.1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  3. Positive and large: the molecules go from a crowded liquid to a gas that fills a huge volume.
Try it: Benzene melts at 5.5 °C with ΔHfus = 9.87 kJ/mol. Find ΔSfus in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
Example 2

Heating a liquid and a solid

liquidsolid

Find ΔS when 100 g of water is heated from 25 °C to 75 °C (c = 4.184 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹).

  1. Kelvin: T1 = 298.15 K, T2 = 348.15 K.
  2. ΔS = m c ln(T2/T1) = (100 g)(4.184 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹) ln(348.15/298.15) = (418.4)(0.1550).
  3. ΔS = +64.9 J/K. Warmer particles move faster and can share their energy in more ways.
Try it: 50.0 g of aluminum (c = 0.897 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹) is heated from 20 °C to 100 °C. Find ΔS in J/K.
Example 3

Expanding or compressing a gas

gas

2.00 mol of an ideal gas expands at constant temperature from 10.0 L to 50.0 L. Find ΔS.

  1. ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) = (2.00)(8.314) ln(50.0/10.0) = (16.63)(1.609).
  2. ΔS = +26.8 J/K. More volume means more possible positions for each molecule (Lesson 10).
Try it: 0.500 mol of ideal gas is compressed at constant temperature from 1.00 atm to 4.00 atm. Find ΔS in J/K (watch the sign).
Example 4

A chemical reaction: making ammonia

reaction

Find ΔS° for N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g) at 25 °C.

  1. Products: 2 S°(NH₃) = 2(192.8) = 385.6 J/K.
  2. Reactants: S°(N₂) + 3 S°(H₂) = 191.6 + 3(130.7) = 583.7 J/K.
  3. ΔS° = 385.6 − 583.7 = −198.1 J/K. Negative: 4 mol of gas become 2 mol, so there are fewer ways to arrange the particles.
Try it: Find ΔS° for CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). S° values: CaCO₃ 91.7, CaO 38.1, CO₂ 213.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Entropy of the surroundings

The surroundings are a huge reservoir at constant temperature, so the heat they exchange counts as reversible. At constant pressure the heat they gain is qsurr = −qsys = −ΔHsys, so:3,5

ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys / T
  • Exothermic (ΔH < 0): heat flows into the surroundings, so ΔSsurr > 0.
  • Endothermic (ΔH > 0): heat is taken from the surroundings, so ΔSsurr < 0.
  • Temperature matters: the same heat has a bigger effect when T is low. Atkins compares it to sneezing: a sneeze in a quiet library (cold surroundings) causes far more disturbance than the same sneeze in a busy street (hot surroundings).5

The entropy of the universe decides

ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr = ΔSsys − ΔHsys/T

ΔSuniv > 0

Spontaneous in the forward direction.

ΔSuniv = 0

At equilibrium: no net change either way (the process is reversible).

ΔSuniv < 0

Nonspontaneous as written; the reverse is spontaneous.

Second-law criterion as stated in OpenStax Chemistry 2e 16.3 and Atkins.3,5

Worked examples of the three situations

Spontaneous

Burning methane at 25 °C

gas

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l): ΔH° = −890.5 kJ, ΔS° = −242.9 J/K. Is it spontaneous at 298 K?

  1. ΔSsys = −242.9 J/K (3 mol of gas become 1 mol of gas plus liquid).
  2. ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T = −(−890 500 J)/298.15 K = +2987 J/K.
  3. ΔSuniv = −242.9 + 2987 = +2744 J/K > 0: spontaneous. The system's entropy falls, but the huge release of heat raises the surroundings' entropy far more.
Equilibrium

Water boiling at 100 °C and 1 atm

liquid ⇌ gas

H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) at 373.15 K: ΔH = +40.7 kJ, ΔSsys = +109.1 J/K.

  1. ΔSsurr = −40 700 J / 373.15 K = −109.1 J/K.
  2. ΔSuniv = 109.1 − 109.1 = 0: equilibrium. Liquid and vapour coexist; neither direction is favoured. That is exactly what "boiling point" means.
Nonspontaneous

Decomposing limestone at 25 °C

solid → gas

CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): ΔH° = +179.2 kJ, ΔS° = +160.2 J/K. Is it spontaneous at 298 K?

  1. ΔSsurr = −179 200 J / 298.15 K = −601.0 J/K.
  2. ΔSuniv = 160.2 − 601.0 = −440.8 J/K < 0: nonspontaneous at room temperature (limestone buildings don't fall apart).
  3. It becomes spontaneous when 160.2 > 179 200/T, i.e. above T = 179 200/160.2 ≈ 1120 K (≈ 845 °C) — which is why lime kilns run so hot.
Try it: Dissolving NH₄NO₃ (a cold pack): ΔH° = +25.7 kJ/mol, ΔS° = +108.7 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find ΔSuniv at 298 K (J/K). Is it spontaneous?

When the entropy of the universe would decrease: heat flowing uphill

The clearest example of a process that would lower the entropy of the universe is heat flowing by itself from a colder object to a hotter one — a cold drink getting even colder while the warm room around it heats up. Energy would be conserved, so the first law allows it. The second law does not.3,13

Nonspontaneous

Could a cold drink cool itself further in a warm room?

heat flow

A drink at 5 °C sits in a room at 25 °C. Suppose 1000 J of heat flowed out of the drink and into the room. Treat both as large enough that their temperatures barely change, so each one's entropy change is q/T.3,8

  1. Kelvin: Tdrink = 278.15 K, Troom = 298.15 K.
  2. The drink loses 1000 J: ΔSdrink = −1000 J / 278.15 K = −3.595 J/K.
  3. The room gains 1000 J: ΔSroom = +1000 J / 298.15 K = +3.354 J/K.
  4. ΔSuniv = −3.595 + 3.354 = −0.241 J/K < 0: nonspontaneous. The same amount of heat changes the entropy of the colder object more (dividing by a smaller T), so moving heat from cold to hot always lowers the entropy of the universe. That is why it never happens on its own. The reverse — the drink warming up — gives ΔSuniv = +0.241 J/K and does happen.
Try it: Suppose 500 J of heat flowed from an ice cube at 0 °C into boiling water at 100 °C. Calculate ΔSuniv in J/K.

Making it happen anyway: the refrigerator

A refrigerator does move heat from its cold inside to the warm kitchen — but not on its own. It needs work from the electricity supply, and that work ends up as extra heat released into the kitchen. The kitchen gains more entropy than the inside loses, so the entropy of the universe still increases.13

Entropy in living things and in chemical reactions

Living things build highly ordered structures — proteins, DNA, cells — from simple, scattered molecules. Doesn't that decrease entropy and break the second law? No: an organism is an open system. It takes in food and oxygen and releases heat, CO₂ and water. The entropy inside it may go down, but the entropy it exports to its surroundings is much larger, so ΔSuniv > 0. Schrödinger famously described organisms as staying ordered by "feeding on" the low entropy of their food.10

Biology 1

Cellular respiration in your body (37 °C)

biological

C₆H₁₂O₆(s) + 6 O₂(g) → 6 CO₂(g) + 6 H₂O(l)    ΔH° = −2802.5 kJ (Lesson 9)

S° (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹): glucose 209.2, O₂ 205.2, CO₂ 213.8, H₂O(l) 70.0.6,7 Is respiration spontaneous at body temperature, 310 K?

  1. System: ΔS°sys = [6(213.8) + 6(70.0)] − [209.2 + 6(205.2)] = 1702.8 − 1440.4 = +262.4 J/K. Seven molecules (one large, solid glucose molecule and 6 O₂) become 12 smaller ones. The number of gas molecules doesn't change (6 O₂ → 6 CO₂); most of the increase comes from one solid glucose molecule becoming six liquid water molecules.
  2. Surroundings: ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T = −(−2 802 500 J)/310 K = +9040 J/K. The heat released warms your body and the air around you.
  3. ΔSuniv = 262 + 9040 ≈ +9300 J/K per mole of glucose — strongly spontaneous. Your cells capture part of this energy as ATP and use it to build ordered molecules; even after that, the total entropy of the universe still rises.
Biology 2

Photosynthesis: entropy decreases — so it needs sunlight

biological

6 CO₂(g) + 6 H₂O(l) → C₆H₁₂O₆(s) + 6 O₂(g)    ΔH° = +2802.5 kJ, ΔS° = −262.4 J/K

  1. This is respiration in reverse, so both signs flip. At 298 K: ΔSsurr = −2 802 500/298.15 = −9400 J/K.
  2. ΔSuniv = −262 − 9400 ≈ −9660 J/K < 0: nonspontaneous on its own: without light to drive it, plants can't turn CO₂ and water into sugar.
  3. Sunlight makes it possible. The plant absorbs high-energy light from the very hot Sun and gives off low-temperature heat; spreading that energy out creates more than enough entropy to pay for the ordered sugar molecule. Counting the Sun and the plant's waste heat as part of the surroundings, ΔSuniv > 0.5
Biology 3

An enzyme reaction: catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide

reaction

2 H₂O₂(l) → 2 H₂O(l) + O₂(g)

Cells make toxic H₂O₂ as a by-product; the enzyme catalase destroys it (that's the fizzing when hydrogen peroxide touches a cut). Data: ΔHf°(H₂O₂, l) = −187.8 kJ/mol, S°(H₂O₂, l) = 109.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.6

  1. ΔH° = 2(−285.8) − 2(−187.8) = −196.0 kJ (exothermic).
  2. ΔS°sys = [2(70.0) + 205.2] − 2(109.6) = +126.0 J/K: a gas (O₂) is produced from liquids.
  3. ΔSsurr = +196 000/298.15 = +657.4 J/K.
  4. ΔSuniv = 126.0 + 657.4 = +783.4 J/K. Both terms are positive, so it is spontaneous at every temperature. The enzyme doesn't change this — it only makes the reaction fast.
Biology 4

Protein folding and the hydrophobic effect

biological

unfolded protein (aq) ⇌ folded protein (aq)

Folding turns a floppy chain into one precise shape, so the protein's entropy drops sharply. Yet proteins fold on their own. The key is the water: around exposed oily (nonpolar) side chains, water molecules are held in an ordered cage. When the chain folds and buries those groups inside, the caged water is released and gains entropy. This hydrophobic effect is a major driving force of folding.11

Entropy ledger: ΔS(protein chain) < 0; ΔS(released water) > 0, offsetting much of that loss; and folding releases heat, so ΔSsurr > 0. Together, ΔSuniv > 0. The same effect makes oil droplets merge in water and helps cell membranes form.

How total entropy increases in these examples
  • Releasing heat to the surroundings (respiration, H₂O₂ breakdown): ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T is large and positive.
  • Making more, smaller molecules — especially gases (glucose + 6 O₂ → 12 smaller molecules; 2 H₂O₂(l) → O₂ gas): ΔSsys > 0.
  • Freeing ordered solvent (the hydrophobic effect): water gains entropy even as the protein loses it.
  • Using an outside energy source (sunlight for photosynthesis, food for living cells) to pay for local decreases in entropy.

Putting it together: four combinations of ΔH and ΔS

Because ΔSuniv = ΔSsys − ΔHsys/T, the signs of ΔH and ΔS — together with temperature — decide spontaneity:3,4

Module wrap-up

You can now follow energy through any process — choose a system, track q and w across its boundary, and use the state functions U and H with Hess's law to calculate changes — and you can predict whether a process will happen on its own by adding up the entropy changes of the system and its surroundings. The next unit combines these into a single quantity, the Gibbs free energy.
