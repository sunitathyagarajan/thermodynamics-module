Lesson 12

The third law: the bottom of the entropy scale

The first law says energy is conserved; the second says the entropy of the universe increases in every spontaneous process. The third law answers a quieter question: is there a zero of entropy? Yes — and that single fact lets chemists tabulate absolute entropies for every substance.

In this lesson

  • State the third law and connect it to microstates (W = 1).
  • Explain why some real crystals keep a "residual entropy" at 0 K.
  • Show how absolute entropies are measured from heat capacities and phase changes.
  • Explain why absolute zero can be approached but never reached.

The third law

The third law of thermodynamics The entropy of a perfect crystal of a pure substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero (0 K).1,3

Walther Nernst arrived at the idea in 1906 from measurements of reactions at low temperature (his "heat theorem"), and Max Planck soon put it in the form above.3 There are three equivalent ways to say it:

  1. Thermodynamic: S → 0 for a perfect crystal as T → 0 K.1
  2. Microscopic: at 0 K a perfect crystal has only one possible arrangement, W = 1, so S = kB ln 1 = 0 (Lesson 10).2
  3. Practical: absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.3,4

Why a perfect crystal has zero entropy

At absolute zero every particle sits in its lowest-energy state, locked in a perfectly regular lattice. There is exactly one way to arrange the system: one microstate. Warm it up and the particles begin to vibrate, the energy can be shared among them in many ways, and W — and therefore S — rises.2,4

Residual entropy: when a crystal isn't perfect

Carbon monoxide

CO molecules are almost symmetric, so as the solid forms they freeze in pointing either way (CO or OC) at random. With 2 choices per molecule, W = 2N:

S = kB ln 2N = N kB ln 2 = R ln 2 = 5.76 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (for 1 mol)

Clayton and Giauque measured a residual entropy of about 4.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ — slightly less, because the orientations aren't perfectly random.7

Ice

Each H atom sits closer to one O atom than the other, and many arrangements obey the rules of hydrogen bonding. Linus Pauling estimated W ≈ (3/2)N, so

S = R ln(3/2) = 3.37 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹

— which matched the 3.4 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ that Giauque and Stout found by comparing measured and calculated entropies of water.5,6

Glasses (amorphous solids) also keep residual entropy, because their particles are frozen in a disordered arrangement. The third law applies strictly only to perfect, pure crystals.3

Measuring absolute entropy

Because the third law fixes S = 0 at 0 K, we can measure the absolute entropy of a substance by warming it from (near) 0 K and adding up every entropy change along the way (Lesson 11):1,3

S(T) = Σ n CP ln(T2/T1)  +  Σ ΔHtrans/Ttrans heating steps in each phase (strictly, the area under a plot of CP/T against T) + one jump for each phase change

Very close to 0 K heat capacities become tiny (they fall off as T³), so the entropy rises smoothly from zero. This is how the S° values in the tables you used in Lesson 11 were obtained.3

Enthalpy has no natural zero — entropy does For enthalpy we had to choose a reference: ΔHf° of elements = 0 by convention (Lesson 9). Entropy needs no such choice: the third law gives a true zero. That's why S° of elements is not zero — S°(O₂, g) = 205.2 and S°(C, graphite) = 5.7 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ — and why entropy tables list absolute values rather than changes.1,8

Why absolute zero can't be reached

The lowest temperatures are reached in steps. In one classic method (adiabatic demagnetization), a magnetic salt is magnetized while held at a low temperature — which lowers its entropy — and then insulated and demagnetized, which cools it. Each cycle cools the sample further, but because the entropy curves for "magnet on" and "magnet off" both go to zero at 0 K, every step is smaller than the last.3,4 Try it:

Experiments have reached about 10−10 K — a tenth of a billionth of a kelvin — but never 0 K itself.4

Why the third law matters for chemistry

  • Absolute entropies (S°) can be measured and tabulated for every substance (Lesson 11).1
  • Reaction entropies then follow from tables: ΔS°rxn = Σn S°(products) − Σm S°(reactants).1
  • Together with enthalpies, they give Gibbs free energies — ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° — which predict whether reactions run and how far (Lesson 13).

Practice

Example

Adding up the absolute entropy of liquid water at 25 °C

Use these contributions for 1 mol of water: heating ice from 0 K to 273.15 K, +38.0 J/K; residual entropy of ice, +3.4 J/K; melting (ΔHfus = 6010 J/mol at 273.15 K); heating the liquid to 298.15 K (CP = 75.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).

  1. Melting: ΔS = 6010 / 273.15 = +22.0 J/K.
  2. Heating the liquid: ΔS = (1)(75.3) ln(298.15/273.15) = 75.3 × 0.0876 = +6.6 J/K.
  3. Add everything: 3.4 + 38.0 + 22.0 + 6.6 = 70.0 J/K.
  4. S°(H₂O, l, 298 K) = 70.0 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ — exactly the table value.8 Without the residual entropy, the heat-capacity measurements alone give 66.6.
