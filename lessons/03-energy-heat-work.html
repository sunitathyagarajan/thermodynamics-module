Lesson 3

Internal energy, heat & work

A system has internal energy, U. It can exchange energy with its surroundings in exactly two ways: as heat, q, or as work, w. This lesson takes each in turn.

In this lesson

  • Describe internal energy at the molecular level for solids, liquids and gases.
  • Define heat; use q = mcΔT and q = nΔH; read a heating curve.
  • Define work; derive and use w = −PextΔV.
  • Apply the chemist's sign conventions for q and w.
  • Identify q and w in examples involving gases, liquids and solids.

Internal energy, U

The internal energy U of a system is the total of all the kinetic and potential energies of all its particles:

Three facts about U
  1. U is a state function. It depends only on the state (T, P, phase, composition), not on history.
  2. We can't measure the absolute value of U — there are too many contributions. We only ever measure changes: ΔU = Ufinal − Uinitial.
  3. For an ideal gas, U depends only on temperature. If an ideal gas's temperature doesn't change, ΔU = 0. (Ideal gas particles don't attract each other, so there's no intermolecular potential energy that changes with volume.)

At the same temperature, 1 mol of steam has about 38 kJ more internal energy than 1 mol of liquid water — that's the energy needed to pull the molecules apart against their attractions.

Heat, q

Heat is energy transferred between a system and its surroundings because of a temperature difference. It always flows spontaneously from hotter to colder. At the molecular level, fast-moving particles of the hot object collide with slower particles of the cold object and pass on some of their kinetic energy.

Heat ≠ temperature Temperature measures how hot something is (the average kinetic energy of its particles). Heat is energy in transit. A bathtub of warm water holds far more thermal energy than a red-hot pin, even though the pin's temperature is higher. And an object never "contains heat" — heat only exists while energy is crossing a boundary.

Heat that changes temperature: q = m c ΔT

q = m × c × ΔT m = mass (g) · c = specific heat capacity (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹) · ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial (°C or K — same size)

The specific heat capacity c is the heat needed to raise 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. Water's is unusually large, which is why oceans moderate climate. Metals have small values — they heat up quickly. Using moles instead, q = n Cm ΔT, where Cm is the molar heat capacity.

Heat that changes phase: q = n ΔH

During melting or boiling, added heat does not raise the temperature. It goes into pulling molecules apart against their attractions (raising potential energy). The heat needed per mole is the enthalpy of fusion (ΔHfus) or vaporization (ΔHvap). For water: ΔHfus = 6.01 kJ/mol at 0 °C and ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol at 100 °C.

Work, w

In physics, work is done when a force moves something through a distance: w = F × d. In chemistry the most common kind is pressure–volume (PV) work: a gas pushing a piston (or the atmosphere) back as it expands, or being pushed in as it is compressed. Other kinds include electrical work (batteries) and stirring work.

Deriving w = −Pext ΔV

Use the external pressure The work depends on the pressure the gas pushes against (Pext), not the gas's own pressure. Two special cases: expansion into a vacuum (Pext = 0) gives w = 0; and a rigid container (ΔV = 0) gives w = 0. If P is in atm and V in L, the work comes out in L·atm; multiply by 101.325 J/(L·atm).

Joule's experiment: work and heat are both just energy

In the 1840s James Joule let a falling weight turn a paddle wheel in an insulated tank of water. No heat was added — yet the water got warmer. Work done on the water raised its internal energy exactly as heating would. This showed heat and work are two forms of the same thing: energy transfer. (His measurements gave the first accurate "mechanical equivalent of heat", about 4.16 J per calorie; today the calorie is defined as exactly 4.184 J.)

Sign conventions — the system's point of view

Chemists always take the system's point of view. Think of the system's energy as a bank account: anything that adds energy to the account is positive; anything that withdraws energy is negative.

Heads up for later courses Some physics and engineering textbooks write ΔU = q − w, defining w as work done by the system. It's the same physics with the opposite sign for w. In chemistry we use ΔU = q + w (IUPAC convention).

Examples in gases, liquids and solids

Here's how q and w show up for each state of matter. Notice a pattern: work is large only when a gas is produced, consumed, expanded or compressed. Solids and liquids barely change volume, so their PV work is tiny.

Gas

Heating air in a balloon

q > 0 (heat absorbed) and the balloon expands, pushing back the atmosphere: w < 0. Both terms matter.

Gas

Pumping up a bicycle tyre

You compress the air: w > 0. The air's U rises so the pump barrel feels warm; it then loses heat to your hand (q < 0).

Liquid → gas

Boiling water

Per mole: q = +40.7 kJ. The steam (30.6 L!) pushes back the atmosphere: w ≈ −3.1 kJ. Here work is noticeable because a gas is formed. (Worked in Lesson 6.)

Liquid

Stirring or heating water in a beaker

Heating: q > 0, w ≈ 0 (volume barely changes), so ΔU ≈ q. Stirring hard: w > 0 from the stirrer — Joule's experiment!

Solid

Heating a block of aluminum

q = +9.4 kJ for 150 g heated by 70 °C, but the block expands by only 0.27 cm³, so w ≈ −0.03 J. Negligible: ΔU ≈ q.

Solid → liquid

Melting ice

q = +6.01 kJ/mol. Ice is less dense than water, so the volume decreases (by 1.6 mL/mol) and the atmosphere does a tiny bit of work on it: w ≈ +0.17 J.
