Lesson 7

Reversible vs irreversible processes

Heat and work are path functions — so how a change is carried out matters. The two extremes are a reversible path (infinitely slow and gentle) and an irreversible one (fast, with a finite push). Comparing them reveals the maximum work a process can deliver.

In this lesson

  • Define reversible and irreversible processes and recognise each.
  • Explain reversible and irreversible work using a piston and a P–V diagram.
  • Explain reversible and irreversible heat transfer.
  • Compare reversible and irreversible work quantitatively — and see why expansion gives maximum work and compression needs minimum work when done reversibly.
  • Explain when solids and liquids can do reversible work, how it compares with gases, and give real examples.

What do "reversible" and "irreversible" mean?

Reversible process

  • Carried out through a series of tiny steps, so the system is always at (or infinitely close to) equilibrium.
  • The driving force is infinitesimal: Pext differs from Pgas by only dP, or the heat source differs in temperature by only dT.
  • Can be reversed at any moment by an infinitesimal change — and both system and surroundings return exactly to their original states.
  • Takes infinitely long: it's an idealisation, a limit we can approach but never reach.1,2

Irreversible process

  • Driven by a finite difference: a big pressure imbalance, a big temperature gap.
  • Happens at a finite speed; during the process the system is not in equilibrium (turbulence, temperature gradients).
  • Can be undone only by doing something that leaves a permanent change in the surroundings.
  • All real processes are irreversible: an egg breaking, a gas rushing into a vacuum, a hot pan cooling.1

Reversible and irreversible work

Remember: w = −PextΔV, and on a P–V diagram |w| is the area under the external-pressure path. To get the most work out of an expanding gas, we want Pext to be as high as possible at every stage — but it can't exceed Pgas, or the gas would be compressed instead. The best we can do is Pext = Pgas − dP all the way: that's the reversible path.3,4

Reversible, isothermal, ideal gas:   wrev = −nRT ln(V2/V1) the area under the smooth isotherm P = nRT/V (derived with calculus: w = −∫P dV)3

How to compare reversible and irreversible work
  1. Same initial and final states (so ΔU is the same for both paths).
  2. Calculate each: irreversible with w = −PextΔV (summed over steps); reversible with w = −nRT ln(V2/V1).
  3. Or compare areas on a P–V diagram: the reversible path hugs the isotherm.

Result: for expansion, |wrev| > |wirr| — reversible gives the maximum work out. For compression, wrev < wirr — reversible needs the minimum work in. Taking a gas around an irreversible cycle always costs net work; a reversible cycle costs nothing.

Reversible and irreversible heat

Heat flows because of a temperature difference. If that difference is finite (a 20 °C block dropped on an 80 °C hot plate), the heat flow is irreversible — you can't make heat flow back from the block to the hot plate by a tiny change. If the difference is only infinitesimal (dT), the flow is reversible: lowering the source temperature by dT would reverse the direction.1,2

Is qrev different from qirr?

It depends on the path, because q = ΔU − w and ΔU is fixed by the states:

  • When work changes with the path (a gas expanding), q changes too. In Example 9 (Lesson 6) the isothermal expansion absorbed qrev = 1717 J but only qirr = 1239 J in one step. Since ΔU = 0, more work out means more heat in: qrev > qirr for expansion.
  • When there is essentially no work (heating water in the widget above), q ≈ ΔU is the same — 25.1 kJ — whether you use one hot plate or a thousand reservoirs. What changes is how much entropy is created.

Reversible heat is special: in the next unit you'll see that entropy is defined as ΔS = qrev/T.2

Reversible work in gases, liquids and solids

Reversibility depends on how a process is carried out, not on what is being changed. A process is reversible when it is carried out quasi-statically (through a continuous series of equilibrium states) with nothing that dissipates energy — no friction, no turbulence, no finite pressure or temperature gap.1,3 Nothing in that definition mentions gases, so solids and liquids can do reversible work too. What changes from phase to phase is:

  1. How much pressure–volume work is available. Gases change volume a lot; solids and liquids barely change at all.
  2. Which other kinds of work matter. Solids and liquids can do reversible elastic, surface and electrical work, which can be large even when their PV work is negligible.

Gases: large, easy-to-see reversible work

Because a gas's volume responds strongly to pressure, the difference between reversible and irreversible work is large — as the animations above showed (3.44 kJ vs 1.86 kJ for the same expansion). For a reversible isothermal change of an ideal gas, w = −nRT ln(V2/V1); for a reversible adiabatic change, TVγ−1 stays constant.3,4

Example 1 · Sound: a nearly reversible process in air gas

A sound wave is a train of tiny compressions and expansions of the air. Each squeeze is too fast for heat to flow out (adiabatic), but so small and smooth that almost no energy is dissipated — so it behaves as a reversible adiabatic process.13

Newton assumed the compressions were isothermal and predicted a speed of sound about 15% too low. Laplace corrected this in 1816 by treating them as adiabatic:14

v = √(γRT/M) = √(1.40 × 8.314 × 293 / 0.02897) ≈ 343 m/s at 20 °C

versus √(RT/M) ≈ 290 m/s for Newton's isothermal assumption. The measured value matches Laplace's.13

Liquids: tiny PV work — unless a gas forms — but other reversible work

Squeezing a liquid takes very little work whether you do it slowly or suddenly (see the compression tool below). But liquids can still take part in substantial reversible work:

Example 2 · Boiling at the boiling point liquid → gas

At 100 °C and 1 atm, liquid water and steam are in equilibrium, so vaporization there is a reversible phase change: cooling by dT would condense the steam again.2,5 Because it makes a gas, it does real PV work: wrev = −PΔV ≈ −nRT = −(1.00 mol)(8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(373 K) = −3.10 kJ per mole (Lesson 6, Example 5).3 Melting ice at 0 °C is reversible too, but its work is only +0.17 J/mol because no gas is involved.

Example 3 · Surface work: stretching a liquid film liquid

Creating new liquid surface takes work, w = γΔA, where γ is the surface tension.8 Pull the sliding wire of a soap-film frame out slowly and the film pulls back with the same force: let go gently and it returns the work. Done slowly, this is reversible work by a liquid.

Numbers: water has γ = 72.0 mJ/m² at 25 °C.9 Spraying 1.00 L of water into droplets 1 µm across creates about 6000 m² of new surface, so the minimum (reversible) work is 0.072 J/m² × 6000 m² ≈ 430 J. Real spray nozzles use far more, because turbulence and friction make the process highly irreversible.

Example 4 · Electrical work from a cell with no gases solids + solutions

A Daniell cell, Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s), involves only a solid metal and aqueous solutions, so its PV work is essentially zero. Yet if it is discharged reversibly — against an opposing voltage only infinitesimally smaller than its own, so the current is tiny — it delivers the maximum electrical work:10

wmax = −nFE° = −(2)(96 485 C/mol)(1.10 V) = −212 kJ per mol Zn

Short-circuit the same cell and w = 0: the energy comes out entirely as heat (like the battery in Lesson 4). Same reaction, same ΔU, very different work — and condensed phases doing a large amount of reversible work. (This maximum work is ΔG, which you'll meet with the second law.)

Solids: elastic work is reversible, plastic work is not

Compressing a solid takes even less PV work than compressing a liquid. Diamond anvil cells can still squeeze solids slowly to over a million atmospheres, where their volumes shrink noticeably — a good approximation to reversible compression.15 The irreversible extreme is shock compression by explosives or projectiles, where a sudden pressure jump heats the solid sharply.16

The most familiar reversible work by a solid, though, is elastic. Stretch a steel wire or spring within its elastic limit and it springs back along the same line, returning the work you put in. Stretch it past its yield point and it deforms plastically: it stays longer, and part of the work turns into heat. That's why a paper clip bent back and forth gets warm.11,12 A rubber band also warms when stretched quickly and cools when it contracts — close to reversible, though real rubber loses a little energy on each cycle (hysteresis).17

How is this calculated?

Work per unit volume is the area under the stress–strain curve.11 Elastic region: stress = E × strain, so work = ½E(strain)². Beyond the yield strain (0.125%) the wire is modelled as yielding at 250 MPa with slight hardening. On release it unloads along a line with the elastic slope, returning ½(stress)²/E per unit volume; the rest of the area is dissipated.12 Multiply by the wire's volume (1.00 × 10⁻⁶ m³) to get joules.

Pressure–volume work for liquids and solids

For a liquid or solid, the volume shrinks almost exactly in proportion to the pressure increase.7,11 On a P–V diagram that's a nearly vertical straight line. The area under a straight line is just (average height) × (width):

Reversible compression

Pext rises gradually from P1 to P2, so the average pressure is about ½(P1 + P2):

wrev ≈ −½(P1 + P2) ΔV

Irreversible (sudden) compression

The full pressure P2 is applied at once:

wirr = −P2 ΔV

When P2 ≫ P1, that's about twice the reversible work — but twice a tiny number is still tiny.

Here ΔV = −κVΔP, where κ (the compressibility) is a property of the material: about 4.6 × 10⁻⁵ atm⁻¹ for water6 and 6 × 10⁻⁷ atm⁻¹ for iron.7 An ideal gas, by contrast, halves its volume when the pressure doubles.

Summary: reversible work in different phases
  • Possible for every phase. Reversibility depends on how the process is carried out (quasi-static, no dissipation), not on whether the substance is a gas, liquid or solid.1,3
  • PV work: large for gases, so reversible and irreversible values differ a lot (thousands of joules). Tiny for solids and liquids (tens of joules or less), so the difference is usually negligible and we set w ≈ 0 — unless a gas is made or consumed, as in boiling (−3.10 kJ/mol).6,7
  • Other work: solids and liquids can do substantial reversible work of other kinds — electrical (up to 212 kJ per mole of zinc in a Daniell cell),10 elastic (a stretched wire or spring)11 and surface work (a liquid film).8
  • Heat: heating a solid or liquid reversibly or irreversibly transfers the same q (= mcΔT ≈ ΔU); the irreversibility shows up as entropy created.2
