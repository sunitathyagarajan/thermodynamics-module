Lesson 1

What is thermodynamics?

Thermodynamics is the science of energy — how it is stored, how it moves from place to place, and how it changes from one form into another.

In this lesson

  • Define thermodynamics and say what it can (and cannot) predict.
  • Review kinetic and potential energy and the units of energy.
  • Meet the laws of thermodynamics at a glance.
  • Distinguish state functions from path functions — the single most useful idea in this module.

What the word means

The name comes from two Greek words: thermē (heat) and dynamis (power). It was born in the 1800s when engineers wanted to know how much useful work a steam engine could get out of burning coal. Today chemists use the same ideas to answer questions such as:

  • How much heat is released when methane burns?
  • Will a reaction happen on its own (is it spontaneous)?
  • How much work can an expanding gas do on a piston?
  • Why does heat always flow from hot to cold, never the reverse?
What thermodynamics does not tell you Thermodynamics tells you whether a process can happen and how much energy is involved — but not how fast. Diamond turning into graphite is thermodynamically favourable, yet your diamond ring is safe for billions of years. Speed is the job of kinetics.

The macroscopic point of view

A drop of water contains about 1021 molecules. Tracking each one is impossible. Thermodynamics instead describes matter using a handful of measurable, bulk properties: pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), amount (n) and energy. Once these are fixed, the state of the sample is fixed.

Energy, briefly

Energy is the capacity to do work or to transfer heat. All forms of energy fall into two families:

Kinetic energy — energy of motion

Ek = ½ m v2

Molecules translate, rotate and vibrate. The faster they move, the hotter the sample. Thermal energy is the kinetic energy of random molecular motion.

Potential energy — energy of position

Ep = m g h (gravity)

In chemistry the important kind is chemical potential energy, stored in the attractions between nuclei and electrons in bonds, and between molecules.

The laws at a glance

Thermodynamics rests on four laws. This module focuses on the first law; the others are previewed here so you can see where it fits.

0 · Zeroth law

If A and B are in thermal equilibrium, and B and C are too, then A and C are as well. This is what makes thermometers work.

1 · First law (this module)

ΔU = q + w

Energy is conserved. It can change form and move between system and surroundings, but it cannot be created or destroyed.

2 · Second law

In any spontaneous process the entropy (dispersal of energy) of the universe increases. Heat flows hot → cold on its own, never the reverse.

3 · Third law

The entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is zero. It gives us a reference point for measuring entropy.

State functions vs path functions

This idea will come up again and again, so it is worth getting right now.

  • A state function depends only on the current state of the system — not on how it got there. Examples: P, V, T, internal energy U, enthalpy H. For a change we write ΔX = Xfinal − Xinitial.
  • A path function depends on how the change happens. Heat (q) and work (w) are path functions. We never write "Δq" or "Δw" — a system doesn't contain heat or work; they only exist while energy is being transferred.
