Lesson 6

Reversible vs irreversible processes

Heat and work are path functions — so how a change is carried out matters. The two extremes are a reversible path (infinitely slow and gentle) and an irreversible one (fast, with a finite push). Comparing them reveals the maximum work a process can deliver.

In this lesson

  • Define reversible and irreversible processes and recognise each.
  • Explain reversible and irreversible work using a piston and a P–V diagram.
  • Explain reversible and irreversible heat transfer.
  • Compare reversible and irreversible work quantitatively — and see why expansion gives maximum work and compression needs minimum work when done reversibly.
  • Decide whether the comparison is meaningful for solids and liquids.

What do "reversible" and "irreversible" mean?

Reversible process

  • Carried out through a series of tiny steps, so the system is always at (or infinitely close to) equilibrium.
  • The driving force is infinitesimal: Pext differs from Pgas by only dP, or the heat source differs in temperature by only dT.
  • Can be reversed at any moment by an infinitesimal change — and both system and surroundings return exactly to their original states.
  • Takes infinitely long: it's an idealisation, a limit we can approach but never reach.

Irreversible process

  • Driven by a finite difference: a big pressure imbalance, a big temperature gap.
  • Happens at a finite speed; during the process the system is not in equilibrium (turbulence, temperature gradients).
  • Can be undone only by doing something that leaves a permanent change in the surroundings.
  • All real processes are irreversible: an egg breaking, a gas rushing into a vacuum, a hot pan cooling.

Reversible and irreversible work

Remember: w = −PextΔV, and on a P–V diagram |w| is the area under the external-pressure path. To get the most work out of an expanding gas, we want Pext to be as high as possible at every stage — but it can't exceed Pgas, or the gas would be compressed instead. The best we can do is Pext = Pgas − dP all the way: that's the reversible path.

Reversible, isothermal, ideal gas:   wrev = −nRT ln(V2/V1) the area under the smooth isotherm P = nRT/V (derived with calculus: w = −∫P dV)

How to compare reversible and irreversible work
  1. Same initial and final states (so ΔU is the same for both paths).
  2. Calculate each: irreversible with w = −PextΔV (summed over steps); reversible with w = −nRT ln(V2/V1).
  3. Or compare areas on a P–V diagram: the reversible path hugs the isotherm.

Result: for expansion, |wrev| > |wirr| — reversible gives the maximum work out. For compression, wrev < wirr — reversible needs the minimum work in. Taking a gas around an irreversible cycle always costs net work; a reversible cycle costs nothing.

Reversible and irreversible heat

Heat flows because of a temperature difference. If that difference is finite (a 20 °C block dropped on an 80 °C hot plate), the heat flow is irreversible — you can't make heat flow back from the block to the hot plate by a tiny change. If the difference is only infinitesimal (dT), the flow is reversible: lowering the source temperature by dT would reverse the direction.

Is qrev different from qirr?

It depends on the path, because q = ΔU − w and ΔU is fixed by the states:

  • When work changes with the path (a gas expanding), q changes too. In Example 9 (Lesson 5) the isothermal expansion absorbed qrev = 1717 J but only qirr = 1239 J in one step. Since ΔU = 0, more work out means more heat in: qrev > qirr for expansion.
  • When there is essentially no work (heating water in the widget above), q ≈ ΔU is the same — 25.1 kJ — whether you use one hot plate or a thousand reservoirs. What changes is how much entropy is created.

Reversible heat is special: in the next unit you'll see that entropy is defined as ΔS = qrev/T.

Can we compare reversible and irreversible work for solids and liquids?

In principle, yes. Any substance can be compressed slowly (reversibly) or suddenly (irreversibly), and the same definitions apply. In practice, the difference is tiny, because solids and liquids are nearly incompressible: even huge pressures squeeze them only slightly, so ΔV — and therefore the work — is very small whichever path you take.

A simple way to see it

For a liquid or solid, the volume shrinks almost exactly in proportion to the pressure increase. On a P–V diagram that's a nearly vertical straight line. The area under a straight line is just (average height) × (width):

Reversible compression

Pext rises gradually from P1 to P2, so the average pressure is about ½(P1 + P2):

wrev ≈ −½(P1 + P2) ΔV

Irreversible (sudden) compression

The full pressure P2 is applied at once:

wirr = −P2 ΔV

When P2 ≫ P1, that's about twice the reversible work — but twice a tiny number is still tiny.

Here ΔV = −κVΔP, where κ (the compressibility) is a property of the material: about 4.6 × 10⁻⁵ atm⁻¹ for water and 6 × 10⁻⁷ atm⁻¹ for iron. An ideal gas, by contrast, halves its volume when the pressure doubles.

Answer: solids and liquids
  • Work: Yes, we can compare reversible and irreversible PV work for solids and liquids, and the irreversible work is larger for compression (by up to ~2×). But both values are so small — tens of joules or less, versus thousands for a gas — that the difference is usually negligible. In practice we set w ≈ 0 for condensed phases.
  • Heat: Heating a solid or liquid reversibly or irreversibly transfers the same q (= mcΔT ≈ ΔU), because there's essentially no work to differ. The irreversibility shows up only as entropy created.
  • Where it does matter: phase changes that make or consume a gas (boiling, sublimation), reactions that change Δngas, and processes at enormous pressures (geology, materials science).
