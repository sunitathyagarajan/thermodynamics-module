Lesson 10

Nature's one-way street: the second law

Perfume spreads through a room but never gathers itself back into the bottle. Hot coffee cools but never reheats on its own. The first law says energy is conserved in both directions — so why does nature only run one way? The answer is entropy.

In this lesson

  • State the second law of thermodynamics in words.
  • Describe entropy as a measure of randomness — the number of ways energy and matter can be arranged.
  • Explain three everyday examples of entropy increasing: diffusion, a gas expanding, and melting.
  • Define microstates and use Boltzmann's formula, S = k ln W, to calculate entropy.

Why does nature only run one way?

Imagine filming each of these and playing the film backwards. You would know instantly that something was wrong — even though the backwards version breaks no rule of energy conservation.

Energy would be conserved in every one of these "backwards films". Processes that run on their own in one direction are called spontaneous; their reverses are not.1 What forbids the backwards films is a second, separate law of nature.

The second law of thermodynamics

The second law In any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe increases.3,6 An older, equivalent form (Clausius): heat never flows on its own from a colder object to a hotter one.4

Entropy, symbol S, is a measure of how many different ways the energy and particles of a system can be arranged. A useful everyday picture is randomness or spread-out-ness: the more ways the particles and their energy can be arranged, the higher the entropy.2,5 The word was coined by Rudolf Clausius in 1865, who summed up thermodynamics as "the energy of the universe is constant; the entropy of the universe tends to a maximum."10

A note on "disorder" You will often hear entropy called "disorder." It's a handy starting picture, but it can mislead (a shuffled deck of cards doesn't have more thermodynamic entropy than a sorted one). Many chemists prefer to think of entropy as the spreading out of energy over more possible arrangements.7 In this lesson "randomness" always means "number of possible arrangements."

Like U and H, entropy is a state function (Lesson 5). Its SI unit is J/K (for one mole, J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).2

How spread out

① Diffusion

A drop of food colouring spreads through water; perfume spreads through a room. Each dye molecule wanders randomly. There are vastly more ways for the molecules to be spread through the whole glass than crowded in one drop, so they end up spread out — and never gather back.1,2,8

② A gas expanding

Remove a partition and gas rushes into the empty half. Each molecule now has twice the space, so there are many more possible arrangements. The reverse — all the gas gathering on one side — is possible in principle but fantastically improbable.5

③ Melting (and dissolving)

In a solid, particles are locked in place. In a liquid they can move past each other, so there are many more arrangements: S(liquid) > S(solid). Dissolving salt or sugar works the same way — the particles spread through the solvent.2,11

A fourth example you've already met: heat flowing from hot to cold (Lesson 3). Spreading the energy from a few fast-moving particles over many slower ones increases the number of ways the energy can be shared.4,6

Microstates: counting the arrangements

To make "number of arrangements" precise, chemists distinguish two levels of description:2,5

Macrostate

What we can observe or measure: temperature, pressure, volume — or, in our simple model, how many particles are in each half of a box.

Microstate

One specific arrangement of every particle (which particle is where, and how the energy is shared). Many different microstates can look exactly the same from the outside — they belong to the same macrostate.

Boltzmann's formula

S = kB ln W W = number of microstates for the macrostate · kB = Boltzmann constant = R/NA = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K

Ludwig Boltzmann connected entropy to probability in 1877; the formula is engraved on his tombstone in Vienna.9 It says that entropy grows with the number of microstates — but only as the logarithm, which keeps the numbers manageable even when W is astronomically large.2,5 Two consequences:

  • If W = 1, then S = k ln 1 = 0. A perfect crystal at absolute zero has only one possible arrangement, so its entropy is zero — the third law of thermodynamics.3
  • Changes: ΔS = kB ln(Wfinal/Winitial). If the number of microstates increases, entropy increases.

For N particles shared between two halves, the number of microstates with n particles on the left is the number of ways to choose which n particles they are:

W = N! / [n! (N − n)!]   ·   total microstates = 2N

What the explorer shows As N grows, the bar chart becomes a sharp spike at "half on each side." For 100 particles, the chance of finding all of them on one side is 1 in 2¹⁰⁰ ≈ 1 in 10³⁰. A real sample has about 10²³ particles, so the spread-out state isn't just likely — it's effectively certain. The second law is a law of overwhelming probability.5

From microstates to moles

When a gas doubles its volume, each molecule has twice as many places to be, so W is multiplied by 2 for every molecule: W2/W1 = 2N. Then

ΔS = kB ln 2N = N kB ln 2 = nR ln 2   ⟹   for 1 mol: ΔS = (8.314)(0.693) = +5.76 J/K More generally, ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1) for an ideal gas at constant temperature (Lesson 11)

Practice calculations

Example 1

Microstates of four particles

Four particles (A, B, C, D) are in a box with two halves. (a) How many microstates are there in total? (b) How many belong to the "2 left, 2 right" macrostate? (c) What is its entropy?

  1. Total: each particle has 2 choices, so 2⁴ = 16 microstates.
  2. 2 left: W = 4!/(2! 2!) = 24/4 = 6 (AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD on the left). The full set is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 for n = 0 to 4.
  3. S = k ln W = (1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K)(ln 6) = (1.381 × 10⁻²³)(1.792) = 2.47 × 10⁻²³ J/K.
  4. 16 microstates; 6 of them are "2–2" (the most probable macrostate, 6/16 = 37.5%); S = 2.47 × 10⁻²³ J/K.
Try it: Six particles, three in each half. How many microstates, W?

…and its entropy, in units of 10⁻²³ J/K?

Example 2

Will the gas gather on one side?

A box contains 10 gas molecules moving randomly. What is the probability that, at a given instant, all 10 are in the left half?

  1. Each molecule is in the left half with probability ½, independently.
  2. All 10 at once: (½)¹⁰ = 1/1024.
  3. P = 1/1024 ≈ 0.098% — rare, but it would happen now and then. For 1 mol (6 × 10²³ molecules) it is (½)^(6 × 10²³): it never happens.
Try it: For 20 molecules, the chance that all are in the left half is 1 in how many?
Example 3

Entropy change when a gas expands

gas

Use microstates to find ΔS when 1.00 mol of ideal gas doubles its volume at constant temperature.

  1. Each of the N = 6.022 × 10²³ molecules has twice as many positions: W2/W1 = 2N.
  2. ΔS = k ln 2N = N k ln 2. Since N k = NAk = R for 1 mol: ΔS = R ln 2.
  3. ΔS = (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(0.693) = +5.76 J/K. Positive: the gas has spread out.
Try it: 2.00 mol of ideal gas triples its volume at constant temperature. Find ΔS in J/K.
