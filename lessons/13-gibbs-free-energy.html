Lesson 13

Gibbs free energy: the second law, seen from the system

To use the second law we had to track the entropy of the system and the surroundings. In the 1870s J. Willard Gibbs showed how to package both into one property of the system alone. That quantity — the Gibbs free energy, G — is the most useful number in chemical thermodynamics.

In this lesson

  • Derive ΔG = ΔH − TΔS from the second law and explain what it means.
  • Calculate ΔG° from ΔH° and ΔS°, or from free energies of formation, and connect ΔG° to the equilibrium constant.
  • Answer: do the surroundings, or the universe, have a Gibbs free energy — and why do we focus on the system?
  • Apply Gibbs free energy to real problems in chemistry, medicine, biology and materials engineering.

Deriving Gibbs free energy

Start from the second law (Lesson 11) and add one condition: constant temperature and pressure — the conditions of an open beaker or a living cell.1,3

  1. A process is spontaneous when the entropy of the universe increases: ΔSuniv = ΔSsys + ΔSsurr > 0.2
  2. At constant T and P, the surroundings' entropy change comes from the heat they receive: ΔSsurr = −ΔHsys/T (Lesson 11).2
  3. Substitute: ΔSuniv = ΔSsys − ΔHsys/T. Everything on the right is now a property of the system.
  4. Multiply through by −T (a negative number, so "> 0" becomes "< 0"): −TΔSuniv = ΔHsys − TΔSsys.
  5. Define the Gibbs free energy G = H − TS. At constant temperature, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.
  6. So ΔGsys = −TΔSuniv. Whenever the universe's entropy rises, the system's free energy falls.
ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = −TΔSuniv constant temperature and pressure (G = H − TS is a state function, because H, T and S are)

What Gibbs free energy means

1 · A direction sign

At constant T and P, processes run "downhill" in G. ΔG < 0: spontaneous. ΔG = 0: equilibrium. ΔG > 0: the reverse is spontaneous.1

2 · Energy that is "free" to do work

−ΔG is the maximum useful (non-expansion) work a process can deliver, reached only when it runs reversibly.3 A battery is the clearest case: ΔG = −nFE, the maximum electrical work (Lesson 7's Daniell cell: −212 kJ).5

3 · Energy minus an "entropy tax"

ΔH is the total heat at constant pressure. Part of it, TΔS, must stay as heat to keep the entropy books balanced; the rest, ΔG, is available for work. When ΔS > 0 the "tax" is a refund: more work is available than ΔH alone suggests.

4 · Equilibrium is the bottom of the hill

As a reaction proceeds, the mixture's G falls until it reaches a minimum. That minimum is equilibrium, where ΔG = 0 and there is no further driving force.1

Example

Burning methane

CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) at 25 °C.

  1. From ΔGf°: [−394.4 + 2(−237.1)] − [−50.5 + 0] = −818.1 kJ.
  2. From ΔH° and ΔS°: −890.5 kJ − (298.15 K)(−0.2429 kJ/K) = −890.5 + 72.4 = −818.1 kJ.
  3. Both routes give ΔG° = −818.1 kJ: strongly spontaneous. Note it is smaller in size than ΔH° — the "entropy tax" is 72.4 kJ, because 3 mol of gas become 1 mol of gas plus liquid.

Try it: Use the table to find ΔG° for N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g) at 25 °C, in kJ.

ΔG and the equilibrium constant

Away from standard conditions, ΔG depends on the reaction quotient Q. At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K, which links ΔG° to how far a reaction goes:1

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q   ⟹   ΔG° = −RT ln K   (K = e−ΔG°/RT)

Example

Predicting the vapor pressure of water at 25 °C

liquid ⇌ gas

For H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂O(g), K is simply the pressure of water vapor (in bar). Use ΔH° = +44.0 kJ and ΔS° = 188.8 − 70.0 = +118.8 J/K.

  1. ΔG° = 44.0 − (298.15)(0.1188) = +8.58 kJ (positive: at 1 bar, liquid water does not boil at 25 °C).
  2. K = e−8580/(8.314 × 298.15) = e−3.461 = 0.0314.
  3. P(H₂O) = 0.0314 bar = 3.1 kPa ≈ 23.5 mmHg. The measured vapor pressure of water at 25 °C is 3.17 kPa (23.8 mmHg) — thermodynamics predicts it from table data alone.7

Do the surroundings have a Gibbs free energy?

Yes — G = H − TS can be written for any body at a definite temperature and pressure. So let's calculate it for the surroundings in the usual situation, where they act as a huge heat bath at constant T and P and simply absorb the heat qsurr = −ΔHsys:3

ΔGsurr = ΔHsurr − TΔSsurr = qsurr − T(qsurr/T) = 0
What this tells us
  • When the surroundings only exchange heat, their Gibbs energy doesn't change. Their whole role — the entropy they gain or lose — is already built into ΔGsys through the −ΔH/T term.
  • If the system also does useful work on the surroundings (a battery lifting a weight, an ATP-driven pump), the surroundings' G rises by that work. But the system's G falls by at least as much, so ΔGsys + ΔGsurr ≤ 0.3
  • Free energy can be moved from place to place as work, and it can be lost in irreversible processes, but it can never be created. Energy is conserved; free energy is not.

Is there a Gibbs free energy of the universe?

Not in a useful sense. Gibbs free energy only has meaning for something held at a definite temperature and pressure by its surroundings. The universe has no single temperature or pressure, and it has no surroundings — it is the one truly isolated system. For an isolated system the governing law is simply ΔS ≥ 0.3

You can formally add ΔGsys + ΔGsurr for a laboratory-sized "universe" at one temperature (the ledger above does). But since ΔGsurr = 0, the sum is just ΔGsys = −TΔSuniv: the entropy law again, in different units. It doesn't add new physics.

What does carry meaning is the bullet above: every spontaneous process uses up some free energy — some ability to do useful work — that can never be recovered. Energy is never lost, but its usefulness is.

Why do we always focus on the system?

It's what we can measure

Calorimeters measure the system's ΔH; tables give its S° and ΔGf°. Nobody can measure the entropy change of the whole room, building and atmosphere.

The surroundings are already counted

At constant T and P, the surroundings' only effect is ΔSsurr = −ΔH/T, which G includes automatically. Atkins calls this "concentrating on the system."3

It matches how chemistry is done

Open beakers, reactors held at set conditions and living cells at 37 °C all run at (nearly) constant temperature and pressure — exactly the conditions for which ΔG is the right test.

One number answers three questions

ΔG tells you whether a process can happen, how far it goes (ΔG° = −RT ln K), and the most useful work it can deliver (−ΔG).1,3

Gibbs free energy in the real world

CChemistry: making ammonia for fertilizer (the Haber–Bosch process)

Fertilizer made from Haber–Bosch ammonia grows the food that feeds nearly half of the world's population.16 The reaction N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g) has ΔH° = −91.8 kJ and ΔS° = −198.1 J/K, so it is favored only at low temperature.6 At 25 °C, ΔG° = −32.8 kJ and K ≈ 6 × 10⁵: products are strongly favored, but the reaction is far too slow to be useful. Heating speeds it up. But because ΔS° < 0, the −TΔS term is positive and grows with temperature, so by 450 °C K has fallen to about 4 × 10⁻⁵.

Industry compromises. An iron catalyst gives a useful rate at a moderate 400–500 °C. Very high pressure (about 150–250 atm) pushes the equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas, and ammonia is removed as it forms.8 Thermodynamics tells engineers what is possible; kinetics tells them how fast.

MMedicine: how tightly does a drug bind?

A drug works by binding to a target protein: drug + protein ⇌ complex. Pharmacologists report the dissociation constant Kd (smaller = tighter binding), and the binding free energy is ΔG°bind = RT ln Kd (with Kd in mol/L). Drug designers improve ΔG by tuning both ΔH (better contacts) and ΔS (releasing ordered water, reducing floppiness).9

MMedicine and biology: fever, pasteurization and sterilization

A folded protein is only marginally stable. For unfolding, ΔG at body temperature is typically only a few tens of kJ/mol: the small difference between a large positive ΔH and a large positive TΔS.10 Because ΔS > 0, raising the temperature makes unfolding spontaneous. That is how heat pasteurizes milk and sterilizes surgical instruments: it unfolds (denatures) the proteins that microbes need. It is also one reason a very high fever (above about 41 °C) is dangerous, because the least stable proteins in the body begin to unfold.

BBiology: how cells drive uphill reactions with ATP

Cells constantly need reactions with ΔG > 0. They pair them with ATP hydrolysis (ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ/mol): when two reactions share an intermediate, their ΔG values simply add (Hess's law for G), and the total can be negative.11 (The prime on ΔG°′ means the biochemical standard state, pH 7.)

Interactive

Coupling a reaction to ATP

Respiration powers it all. Oxidizing glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O(l), has ΔG° ≈ −2880 kJ/mol (from Lessons 9 and 11: −2802.5 − 298.15 × 0.2624). If a cell makes about 32 ATP per glucose, that stores 32 × 30.5 ≈ 980 kJ — roughly a third of the free energy released, under standard conditions. Inside real cells, ΔG for ATP hydrolysis is closer to −50 kJ/mol, because concentrations are far from standard (ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q).11

EMaterials and engineering: extracting metals (the Ellingham diagram)

Metallurgists plot ΔG° for forming each metal oxide (per mole of O₂) against temperature. A substance whose line lies lower can take oxygen away from one whose line lies higher. Carbon's line (2 C + O₂ → 2 CO) slopes downward because making CO gas increases entropy — so at high enough temperature, carbon (coke) can reduce iron ore in a blast furnace.12,13

Aluminum can't be made with carbon at any practical temperature (~2000 °C would be needed), so it is produced by electrolysis instead.

EEngineering: the hydrogen fuel cell

A fuel cell turns H₂(g) + ½ O₂(g) → H₂O(l) directly into electrical work. Its limits come straight from ΔG:5,14

  • Maximum voltage: E° = −ΔG°/(nF) = 237 100 / (2 × 96 485) = 1.23 V.
  • Maximum efficiency: ΔG°/ΔH° = 237.1/285.8 = 83%. The other 17% (TΔS, because 1.5 mol of gas become liquid) must leave as heat.
  • A heat engine burning the same hydrogen is limited instead by the temperatures it works between (the Carnot limit), so at low operating temperatures a fuel cell's limit is higher.14

EMaterials: are diamonds forever?

C(diamond) → C(graphite) has ΔG° = −2.9 kJ/mol at 25 °C, so diamond is thermodynamically unstable — it "should" turn into graphite.6 It doesn't, because breaking diamond's bonds is extremely slow: a reminder that ΔG says whether, not how fast (Lesson 11).

Module wrap-up

You have followed energy from a single cup of coffee to blast furnaces, fuel cells and living cells. The tools are always the same: choose a system, track q and w across its boundary, use the state functions U, H, S and G to calculate changes along whatever path is easiest, and let the entropy of the universe — or, at constant T and P, the Gibbs energy of the system — tell you which way nature will go.
