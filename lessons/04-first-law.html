Lesson 4

The first law of thermodynamics

Energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only be transferred or converted from one form to another. For a closed system, this law of conservation becomes one short equation.

In this lesson

  • State the first law in words and as ΔU = q + w.
  • Use an interactive simulator to see how heat and work change U under different conditions.
  • See why ΔU is path-independent while q and w are not.
  • Recognise the special cases: constant volume, adiabatic, isothermal, isolated and cyclic.
  • Connect ΔU to enthalpy, ΔH.

The statement

ΔU = q + w change in internal energy of the system = heat added to the system + work done on the system

Since energy is conserved, whatever the system gains, the surroundings lose:

ΔUsurroundings = −ΔUsystem   ⟹   ΔUuniverse = 0

How does the simulator calculate this?

For a monatomic ideal gas, U = 3⁄2nRT, so ΔU = 3⁄2nRΔT (you don't need to memorise this — it comes from kinetic theory). ① Rigid: w = 0, ΔU = q. ② Constant P: w = −PΔV = −nRΔT, so q = ΔU − w = 5⁄2nRΔT: exactly 40% of the heat leaves again as work. ③ Insulated: q = 0, ΔU = w; the volume follows TV2/3 = constant. ④ Water bath keeps T = 300 K, so ΔU = 0 and q = −w; the gas expands in one step against Pext = Pfinal.

What to notice
  • In ① and ② the same heat gives different temperature rises: at constant pressure some of the energy leaves as work.
  • In ③ the gas heats up with no heat added — work alone raises U. (This is why a bike pump gets hot, and how diesel engines ignite fuel.)
  • In ④ the gas does work but its U doesn't change: every joule of work is "paid for" by heat drawn in from the bath.

Connecting to enthalpy, H

Most chemistry happens in open beakers at constant atmospheric pressure, where the system can do PV work. To make life easier, chemists define enthalpy:

H = U + PV   ⟹   ΔH = ΔU + PΔV = qP  (constant P) for reactions of ideal gases at constant T and P: ΔH = ΔU + ΔngasRT

ΔH and ΔU differ only by the PV work. For reactions involving only solids and liquids, ΔV ≈ 0 and so ΔH ≈ ΔU. They differ noticeably only when the number of moles of gas changes.

No free lunch: perpetual motion

Over and over, inventors have claimed machines that produce work forever with no energy input. For a machine running in a cycle, ΔU = 0, so w = −q. With no heat input (q = 0), the work output must be zero too.

The first law says you can't get out more energy than you put in. (The second law adds that you can't even break even — but that's a story for the next unit.)
