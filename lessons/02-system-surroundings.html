Lesson 2

System & surroundings

Before you can track energy, you must decide what you are tracking it for. Thermodynamics splits the universe into two parts: the piece you care about, and everything else.

In this lesson

  • Define system, surroundings, boundary and universe.
  • See how the choice of system changes the description of the same event.
  • Classify systems as open, closed or isolated.
  • Describe exothermic and endothermic processes from the point of view of both system and surroundings.

Definitions

  • System — the part of the universe we choose to study (e.g. the reactants and products of a reaction, the gas inside a cylinder).
  • Surroundings — everything else that can exchange energy with the system (the beaker, the water bath, the air in the room…).
  • Boundary — the real or imaginary surface that separates them. Energy (and sometimes matter) is counted as it crosses the boundary.
  • Universe — system + surroundings.
Energy bookkeeping across the boundary Because energy is conserved, any energy the system gains is lost by the surroundings, and vice versa:
ΔEsystem = −ΔEsurroundings   ⟹   ΔEuniverse = 0

You choose the system

The system is not fixed by nature — it is a choice you make to simplify the problem. The same event looks different depending on where you draw the boundary. Try it with a cup of hot coffee sitting on a table in a room.

Tip for problems In chemistry problems, the system is usually the chemical reaction itself (the reactants turning into products), and the solvent, container and air are the surroundings. When a problem says "the reaction releases heat," it means q for the reaction is negative.

Exothermic vs endothermic

When a reaction happens in a beaker, heat flows between the reacting chemicals (the system) and the water and air around them (the surroundings). The direction of flow gives the process its name:

Exothermic: heat flows out of the system

qsys < 0. The surroundings get warmer. Examples: combustion, hand warmers (iron rusting), freezing water, dissolving CaCl2.

Endothermic: heat flows into the system

qsys > 0. The surroundings get colder. Examples: instant cold packs (NH4NO3 dissolving), melting ice, boiling water, photosynthesis.

Common confusion "The cold pack feels cold, so it must have lost heat." No! The cold pack (system) absorbs heat from your hand (surroundings). Your hand loses heat, which is why it feels cold. Always ask: which way is heat crossing the boundary of the system?
